# Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020–2025 — Children Ages 2 Through 4

Applies to: children from 24 to 60 months (2 through 4 years). Source: U.S. Department of Agriculture and U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, *Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2020–2025*, Chapter 3: Children and Adolescents, December 2020.

## The overall pattern

From age 2, children should follow the same healthy dietary pattern as older children and adults, scaled to their calorie needs: plenty of vegetables and fruits, whole grains, dairy or fortified soy alternatives, a variety of protein foods, and oils, while limiting added sugars, saturated fat and sodium.

## Calorie needs

Estimated energy needs for children 2 through 4 range from **about 1,000 to 1,600 calories per day**: roughly 1,000–1,400 for 2–3-year-olds and 1,200–1,600 for 4-year-olds, with active children and boys toward the upper end. Individual children vary; growth on the growth chart, not a calorie count, is the practical measure of whether intake is right.

## Daily amounts in a 1,000–1,400 calorie pattern (typical for ages 2–3)

- **Vegetables: 1 to 1½ cups**, spread across dark green, red/orange, beans/peas/lentils, starchy and other vegetables over the week
- **Fruits: 1 to 1½ cups**, mostly whole fruit rather than juice
- **Grains: 3 to 5 ounce-equivalents**, at least half whole grains
- **Dairy: 2 to 2½ cups** — from age 2, low-fat or fat-free milk and yoghurt; cheese; fortified soy alternatives
- **Protein foods: 2 to 4 ounce-equivalents**, including seafood about 2–4 ounces per week (lower-mercury choices), beans, eggs, poultry and lean meats
- **Oils: 3 to 4 teaspoons**

A 1,600-calorie pattern (some 4-year-olds) includes about 2 cups vegetables, 1½ cups fruit, 5 ounce-equivalents of grains, 2½ cups dairy, 5 ounce-equivalents of protein foods and 5 teaspoons of oils.

## Limits

- **Added sugars: less than 10% of calories per day** starting at age 2 — for a 1,200-calorie diet that is under 30 grams (about 7 teaspoons). Sugar-sweetened beverages, sweets, sweetened cereals and flavoured yoghurts are the main sources.
- **Saturated fat: less than 10% of calories per day** starting at age 2, which is why the switch from whole to low-fat milk is recommended at 2.
- **Sodium: less than 1,200 mg per day for ages 1–3 and less than 1,500 mg per day for ages 4–8** (Chronic Disease Risk Reduction intake). Processed meats, cheese, bread, snacks and restaurant foods are the main sources.

## Drinks

Water and plain low-fat milk. 100% juice, if any, should be limited to 4 ounces a day at 2–3 years and 4–6 ounces at 4–5 years. No sugar-sweetened beverages and no caffeine.

## Nutrients of public health concern

Across ages 2–4, intakes of **dietary fibre, potassium, vitamin D and calcium** are commonly below recommendations, while **added sugars, saturated fat and sodium** are commonly above. Vegetables, fruits, beans and whole grains address the fibre and potassium gap; milk and fortified alternatives address calcium and vitamin D.

## Portion sizes and appetite

Preschoolers need child-sized portions — roughly a quarter to a half of an adult portion — and their appetite varies from meal to meal and day to day. Growth slows after age 2, so intake often appears to drop; this is normal. Offer regular meals and 1–2 snacks, let the child decide how much to eat from what is offered, and keep mealtimes free of pressure and screens.

## Continuing safety points

Choking hazards remain a concern until about age 4: keep cutting grapes and hot dogs, avoid whole nuts, popcorn and hard candy, and supervise eating.
