# Water and Hydration for Infants and Young Children

Applies to: children from 6 to 60 months (with notes on the first 6 months). Source: Healthy Eating Research consensus statement *Healthy Beverage Consumption in Early Childhood* (2019), endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics, American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry, Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics and American Heart Association; water intake reference values from the Dietary Reference Intakes (2005).

## Birth to 6 months: no water

Healthy infants under 6 months get all the fluid they need from breast milk or properly prepared formula, even in hot weather; offer more frequent feeds instead. **Giving water to young infants can be dangerous** — it fills a small stomach without nutrients, can reduce milk intake, and in larger amounts can dilute blood sodium (water intoxication) and cause seizures. Formula must never be diluted beyond the label instructions.

## 6 to 12 months: introduce small amounts

When complementary foods begin at about 6 months, **small amounts of plain water — about 4 to 8 ounces (½ to 1 cup, 120–240 ml) a day** — can be offered in an open cup or straw cup with meals. This practises cup skills and helps with the firmer stools that come with solid foods. Breast milk or formula remains the main drink; water should not replace milk feeds.

## 1 to 3 years

**About 1 to 4 cups (240–960 ml) of plain water a day**, alongside about 2–3 cups (16–24 oz) of whole milk in the second year. Total water from all sources (food and drinks) for 1–3-year-olds is about 1.3 litres a day in the Dietary Reference Intakes; food and milk supply a good part of that. Needs rise with heat, activity and illness.

## 4 to 5 years

**About 1½ to 5 cups (360–1,200 ml) of water a day**, with about 2–2½ cups of low-fat milk. Total water from all sources is about 1.7 litres a day at 4–8 years.

## Which water

Tap water from a safe public supply is appropriate. Fluoridated tap water helps prevent tooth decay; families using well water or non-fluoridated bottled water should discuss fluoride with a dentist or clinician. Water should be **plain** — no sugar, flavourings, sweeteners or "vitamin" additives. Sparkling water is acceptable for older toddlers but has no advantage.

## How to encourage water

Offer water at every meal and snack and keep a cup within reach during play. Serve water in an open cup or straw cup rather than a bottle from 12 months; **do not put a child to bed with any drink other than water** (and preferably nothing), because milk or juice pooling on the teeth overnight causes decay. Model drinking water yourself.

## Drinks that do not count as good hydration

Juice, sweetened drinks, flavoured milks, sports drinks and toddler milks add sugar and calories; caffeinated drinks are not appropriate at any of these ages. During vomiting or diarrhoea, an **oral rehydration solution** — not juice, soda or sports drinks — is the appropriate fluid, and a clinician should be consulted for infants.

## Signs of dehydration

Fewer wet nappies or trips to the toilet, dark urine, dry lips and mouth, no tears when crying, sunken eyes or soft spot, unusual sleepiness or irritability. Infants and toddlers dehydrate quickly; seek medical advice promptly if these signs appear, especially with fever, vomiting or diarrhoea.
