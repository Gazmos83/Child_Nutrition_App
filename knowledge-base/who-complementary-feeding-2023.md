# WHO Guideline on Complementary Feeding, 6–23 Months (2023)

Applies to: infants and young children from 6 to 23 months of age. Source: World Health Organization, *Guideline for complementary feeding of infants and young children 6–23 months of age*, 2023.

## What complementary feeding means

Complementary feeding is the period when other foods and liquids are given alongside breast milk (or infant formula). It begins at about 6 months (180 days) of age, when breast milk alone no longer meets an infant's energy and nutrient needs, and it continues until the child is fully eating family foods. Starting complementary foods before 6 months is not recommended; starting much later than 6 months increases the risk of growth faltering and iron deficiency.

## Continue breastfeeding

WHO recommends that breastfeeding continue up to 2 years of age or beyond while complementary foods are introduced. Breast milk remains an important source of energy, protein, fat and many micronutrients during the whole 6–23 month period. For infants who are not breastfed, an age-appropriate milk (infant formula in the first year) should be given in addition to complementary foods.

## Age of introduction and readiness

At around 6 months most infants can sit with support, control their head, open their mouth when food is offered and move food from the front of the mouth to the back to swallow it. These developmental signs, together with the age of about 6 months, indicate readiness for solid foods. Readiness does not mean the infant is ready to stop breastfeeding.

## Which foods to give every day

- **Animal-source foods** — meat, poultry, fish, eggs and dairy — should be eaten every day. They are the richest sources of iron, zinc, vitamin B12 and high-quality protein, the nutrients most often lacking between 6 and 23 months.
- **Fruits and vegetables** should be eaten every day, including dark-green leafy and orange vegetables and fruits for vitamin A.
- **Pulses, nuts and seeds** (as smooth pastes or ground, never whole for young children) are encouraged, especially when animal-source foods are limited.
- **Starchy staples** (rice, maize, wheat, potatoes, cassava) should not make up most of the diet; a diet dominated by starchy porridges is low in iron, zinc and protein.

Dietary diversity matters: WHO's indicator of a minimally diverse diet is at least five of eight food groups in a day (breast milk; grains, roots and tubers; pulses, nuts and seeds; dairy; flesh foods; eggs; vitamin A-rich fruits and vegetables; other fruits and vegetables).

## Foods and drinks to avoid

- **Sugar-sweetened beverages and 100% fruit juice** should not be given to children 6–23 months.
- **Foods high in sugar, salt and trans fats** — sweets, confectionery, salty snacks, sugary cereals — should be avoided. Added sugars and salt are not needed and displace nutrient-dense foods.
- **Non-sugar sweeteners** should not be given to children under 2 years.

## How often and how much

- **6–8 months:** 2–3 meals of complementary foods per day, starting with 2–3 tablespoons per meal and increasing gradually, plus breast milk on demand. Foods should be thick enough to stay on a spoon — pureed, mashed or semi-solid.
- **9–11 months:** 3–4 meals per day, about half of a 250 ml cup per meal, with finely chopped or mashed foods and soft finger foods the infant can pick up.
- **12–23 months:** 3–4 meals per day, about three-quarters to a full 250 ml cup per meal, plus 1–2 nutritious snacks, eating family foods cut or mashed as needed.

Non-breastfed children need an additional 1–2 meals per day and about 1–2 cups of milk per day.

## Responsive feeding

Feed infants directly and help older children feed themselves. Feed slowly and patiently, encourage without forcing, respond to hunger and fullness cues, and minimise distractions. Never punish or reward with food. Talk to the child during meals with eye contact. Responsive feeding supports self-regulation of intake and healthy growth.

## Hygiene and safety

Wash hands before preparing food and feeding. Serve food immediately after preparation, store leftovers safely, use clean utensils and avoid feeding bottles for complementary foods, which are hard to clean.
