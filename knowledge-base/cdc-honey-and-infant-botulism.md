# CDC Guidance: Honey and Infant Botulism

Applies to: infants from birth to 12 months. Source: Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, *Botulism — Prevention* and *Infant Botulism* pages, 2024; consistent with American Academy of Pediatrics guidance.

## The rule

**Do not give honey to a child younger than 12 months.** This includes raw honey, pasteurised honey, honey used as a sweetener in foods or drinks, honey on a pacifier, honey-containing cereals or snacks made at home, and "honey" cough remedies. Honey is not safe for infants in any form or amount.

## Why

Honey can contain spores of the bacterium *Clostridium botulinum*. In children and adults the mature gut prevents these spores from growing. In infants under about 1 year the intestine is not yet mature enough to stop the spores from germinating; the bacteria then grow in the infant's gut and produce a toxin that causes **infant botulism** — a serious illness that paralyses muscles.

Pasteurisation does not reliably destroy botulism spores, so **pasteurised or "baby-safe" honey is not safe either**. Honey baked into foods is also not considered safe for infants.

## What infant botulism looks like

Symptoms usually begin 3 to 30 days after exposure and include:

- Constipation (often the first sign)
- Weak cry, weak suck and difficulty feeding
- Loss of head control, floppiness ("floppy baby")
- Drooping eyelids, reduced facial expression
- Lethargy and reduced movement
- Difficulty breathing in severe cases

**Infant botulism is a medical emergency.** A parent who notices these signs should seek care immediately. With prompt treatment (an antitoxin and supportive care) most infants recover fully.

## Other sources

Honey is the one identifiable *food* source of botulism spores in infants, but spores are also present in soil and dust; most cases have no identified food source. Corn syrup was once thought to be a risk and is no longer considered one, but it offers no benefit and is not recommended for infants either.

## After 12 months

After the first birthday, honey can be given like any other sweetener. Note that dietary guidelines advise **avoiding added sugars of all kinds, including honey, for children under 2 years**, so honey is still best kept to a minimum in the second year.

## Related safety rules for infants

Alongside the no-honey rule, infants should not be given unpasteurised milk, juice or cheese, or raw or undercooked eggs, meat, fish or sprouts, because of infection risk. These are covered in the food-safety guidance.
