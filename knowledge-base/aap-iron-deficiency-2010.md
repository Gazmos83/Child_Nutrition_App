# AAP Clinical Report: Diagnosis and Prevention of Iron Deficiency in Infants and Young Children, 0–3 Years (2010)

Applies to: infants and children from birth to 36 months. Source: American Academy of Pediatrics, Committee on Nutrition, *Diagnosis and Prevention of Iron Deficiency and Iron-Deficiency Anemia in Infants and Young Children (0–3 Years of Age)*, Pediatrics, 2010.

## Why iron matters in the first three years

Iron deficiency is the most common nutrient deficiency in young children. Iron is needed for haemoglobin, for growth, and for brain development; iron deficiency in infancy — even without anaemia — is associated with lasting effects on cognition and behaviour. The period of highest risk is 6 to 24 months, when iron needs are high relative to body size and dietary iron can be low.

## Recommended iron intakes

- **0–6 months:** about 0.27 mg/day (Adequate Intake). Healthy term infants are born with iron stores that, together with breast milk, meet needs for roughly the first 4–6 months.
- **7–12 months:** 11 mg/day (RDA). This is the highest requirement per kilogram of body weight at any age.
- **1–3 years:** 7 mg/day (RDA).

## Breastfed infants

Breast milk is low in iron. Term infants who are exclusively breastfed, or who receive more than half their feeds as breast milk, should receive an **oral iron supplement of 1 mg/kg per day starting at 4 months of age**, continued until iron-containing complementary foods (iron-fortified cereals, pureed meats) are established in the diet. Introducing iron-rich complementary foods at about 6 months is an essential part of meeting needs.

## Formula-fed infants

Infants fed standard iron-fortified infant formula receive adequate iron from the formula and do not need a separate supplement. Low-iron formulas are not recommended.

## Preterm infants

Preterm infants have smaller iron stores and grow faster. Preterm infants who are breastfed should receive **2 mg/kg per day of iron from 1 month of age through 12 months**; preterm infants on iron-fortified formula usually receive enough from the formula but may still need a supplement.

## Complementary foods and iron

From 6 months, offer iron-rich foods every day: pureed meat, poultry and fish; iron-fortified infant cereals; and, later, legumes and eggs. Vitamin C-rich fruits and vegetables eaten at the same meal increase the absorption of plant (non-haem) iron. Iron-fortified cereal, meat and legumes are among the most valuable first foods.

## Cow's milk

Whole cow's milk should **not** be given as a drink before 12 months. It is low in iron, can cause small intestinal blood loss in infants, and its calcium and casein reduce iron absorption. After 12 months, limit cow's milk to about 16–24 ounces (480–720 ml) per day; toddlers who drink much larger amounts of milk are at high risk of iron deficiency because milk displaces iron-rich foods.

## Screening

The AAP recommends **universal screening for anaemia with a haemoglobin measurement at about 12 months of age**, together with an assessment of risk factors (prematurity, low birth weight, exclusive breastfeeding beyond 4 months without supplementation, early cow's milk, high milk intake, low intake of iron-rich foods, feeding problems, poor growth). Children with risk factors should be screened again or tested further as clinically indicated.

## Treatment

Confirmed iron-deficiency anaemia is treated with oral iron prescribed by a clinician, with follow-up to confirm the response. Parents should not start high-dose iron on their own; excess iron is toxic to young children and iron supplements must be stored out of reach.
