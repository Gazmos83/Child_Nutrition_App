# AAP Policy Statement: Fruit Juice in Infants, Children, and Adolescents (2017)

Applies to: children from birth through 5 years (and older). Source: American Academy of Pediatrics, Heyman MB, Abrams SA, Section on Gastroenterology, Hepatology, and Nutrition, Committee on Nutrition, *Fruit Juice in Infants, Children, and Adolescents: Current Recommendations*, Pediatrics, 2017.

## Key message

Fruit juice offers no nutritional benefit over whole fruit for infants and young children, and it carries real downsides: it is high in sugar and calories, low in fibre, promotes tooth decay, and can displace milk and nutrient-rich foods. Whole fruit — mashed, pureed or cut safely — is the preferred way for children to get fruit.

## Infants under 12 months

**Fruit juice should not be given to infants under 1 year of age** unless clinically indicated by a clinician (for example, in the management of constipation). Juice adds sugar and calories, contributes to tooth decay, and can lead to diarrhoea, poor weight gain or excessive weight gain. Breast milk or infant formula, and from 6 months small amounts of water, are the only drinks an infant needs.

## Toddlers 1 to 3 years

If juice is given at all, limit **100% fruit juice to no more than 4 ounces (120 ml) per day**. It should be offered only in a cup as part of a meal or snack — **never in a bottle or an easily transportable covered cup** that allows the child to sip throughout the day, and **never at bedtime**. Prolonged exposure of the teeth to sugar is the main cause of early childhood dental caries.

## Children 4 to 6 years

Limit 100% fruit juice to **4–6 ounces (120–180 ml) per day**. Juice should count toward, not add to, the day's fruit servings, and whole fruit is still preferred.

## Which juices

Only pasteurised 100% fruit juice is acceptable; **unpasteurised juice products are unsafe for children** because of the risk of *E. coli*, *Salmonella* and other infections. "Fruit drinks", "juice cocktails" and sweetened beverages are not juice and should be avoided. Grapefruit juice can interact with several medications; families should check with a pharmacist.

## Juice and health problems

Excessive juice intake is associated with:

- **Dental caries**, especially when sipped from bottles or cups over long periods.
- **Chronic diarrhoea, bloating and abdominal pain**, from the sorbitol and fructose in some juices (apple, pear, prune).
- **Poor weight gain** when juice replaces milk and food, or **excess weight gain** when it adds calories.
- **Malnutrition** when it displaces protein, fat, iron, calcium and zinc from the diet.

## Practical guidance for parents

Offer water and milk as the everyday drinks. Serve whole fruit at meals and snacks. If juice is used, dilute it or keep to the daily limit, serve it in an open cup with a meal, and do not use it to soothe or as a bedtime drink. Children should not be given juice to treat dehydration during illness; oral rehydration solution is appropriate for that purpose.
