# AAP Clinical Report: Early Nutrition and the Prevention of Food Allergy and Atopic Disease (2019)

Applies to: infants from about 4 to 12 months. Source: American Academy of Pediatrics, Greer FR, Sicherer SH, Burks AW, Committee on Nutrition and Section on Allergy and Immunology, *The Effects of Early Nutritional Interventions on the Development of Atopic Disease in Infants and Children*, Pediatrics, 2019; incorporating the NIAID 2017 Addendum Guidelines for the Prevention of Peanut Allergy.

## The change in thinking

Earlier advice to delay allergenic foods (peanut, egg, fish, dairy) until 1–3 years of age did **not** prevent allergy and may have increased it. Current evidence shows that **there is no reason to delay the introduction of allergenic foods beyond 4–6 months of age**, once an infant is developmentally ready for complementary foods, and that for peanut, *early* introduction actively reduces the risk of peanut allergy in high-risk infants.

## Breastfeeding and maternal diet

Exclusive breastfeeding for the first 3–4 months protects against eczema and, for any duration of breastfeeding, against wheezing in the first 2 years. There is **no evidence that a mother avoiding allergenic foods during pregnancy or breastfeeding prevents allergy** in her child; such restriction is not recommended. Hydrolysed formulas are not recommended for allergy prevention in infants who are not breastfed.

## When to start allergenic foods

Allergenic foods can be introduced **alongside other complementary foods from about 6 months (not before 4 months)**, one new food at a time, a few days apart, so that any reaction can be identified. Common allergenic foods include peanut, egg, cow's milk products (yoghurt, cheese — not milk as a drink before 12 months), tree nuts, wheat, soy, fish, shellfish and sesame. They should be offered in **infant-safe forms**: smooth peanut butter thinned with breast milk, formula or water or mixed into puree; well-cooked scrambled or mashed egg; yoghurt; flaked cooked fish. **Whole peanuts, whole tree nuts and spoonfuls of thick nut butter are choking hazards** and must never be given to young children.

## Peanut: the NIAID risk-based guidance (2017)

- **Infants with severe eczema and/or egg allergy (high risk):** introduce peanut-containing foods **as early as 4–6 months**, *after* evaluation by a clinician — peanut-specific IgE blood test and/or skin-prick test — and, if indicated, a supervised first feeding in the clinic. Once tolerated, about 2 grams of peanut protein (roughly 2 teaspoons of peanut butter) three times a week helps maintain tolerance.
- **Infants with mild to moderate eczema:** introduce peanut-containing foods **around 6 months**, at home, in line with family preferences.
- **Infants with no eczema or food allergy:** introduce peanut-containing foods **freely with other complementary foods**, in an age-appropriate form.

## Egg

Cooked egg can be introduced around 6 months. Emerging evidence suggests early introduction of cooked egg reduces egg allergy. Raw or undercooked egg should not be given to infants.

## How to introduce safely

Offer the first taste of a new allergenic food at home, early in the day, when the child is well, and watch for 2 hours. Signs of an allergic reaction include hives, swelling of the lips or face, vomiting, widespread rash, coughing, wheezing or difficulty breathing, or unusual paleness and floppiness. **Breathing difficulty, swelling of the tongue or throat, or floppiness is an emergency — call emergency services immediately.** Milder reactions should be discussed with a clinician before offering the food again.

## Once a food is tolerated

Keep the food in the diet regularly — roughly a few times a week — to maintain tolerance. Stopping for long periods after early introduction may lose the protective effect.

## Infants with a diagnosed food allergy

Infants already diagnosed with a food allergy, or with a sibling with peanut allergy, should have an individualised plan from their clinician or an allergist. Nothing in this guidance replaces that plan.
