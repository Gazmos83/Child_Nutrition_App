# WHO/UNICEF Recommendations on Breastfeeding and Infant Feeding

Applies to: infants and young children from birth to 24 months. Source: World Health Organization and UNICEF infant and young child feeding recommendations (Global Strategy for Infant and Young Child Feeding and subsequent WHO guidance), as published on WHO's breastfeeding pages, 2021.

## Exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months

WHO recommends exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months of life. Exclusive means the infant receives only breast milk — no other food or drink, not even water — with the exception of oral rehydration solution, drops or syrups of vitamins, minerals or medicines when medically indicated. Breast milk provides all the energy, fluid and nutrients a healthy full-term infant needs during this period, and its antibodies protect against common childhood illnesses such as diarrhoea and pneumonia.

## Start within the first hour

Breastfeeding should begin within the first hour after birth. Early skin-to-skin contact and early initiation help establish milk supply and are associated with lower newborn mortality. Colostrum, the first milk, is rich in antibodies and is the ideal first food.

## Feed on demand

Infants should be breastfed on demand — as often as the child wants, day and night — typically 8 to 12 times in 24 hours in the early weeks. Feeding on demand supports milk production and lets the infant regulate intake. Bottles, teats and pacifiers are discouraged while breastfeeding is being established.

## Continue to 2 years or beyond

From 6 months, nutritionally adequate and safe complementary foods should be introduced while breastfeeding continues up to 2 years of age or beyond. In the second year, breast milk can still provide roughly one-third or more of a child's energy needs and a large share of protein, vitamin A and other nutrients.

## Infants who are not breastfed

When an infant is not breastfed, or is partially breastfed, a suitable breast-milk substitute — a commercial infant formula — is the appropriate milk during the first 12 months. Formula should be prepared according to WHO safe-preparation guidance and given by cup or bottle with careful hygiene. Home-made formulas, diluted animal milks and plant-based drinks are not suitable substitutes for breast milk or infant formula in the first year.

## Vitamin and mineral supplementation

Breast milk is low in vitamin D; many national guidelines (for example the American Academy of Pediatrics) recommend a daily vitamin D supplement of 400 IU for breastfed infants from the first days of life. Breastfed infants born at term have adequate iron stores for about the first 6 months, after which iron-rich complementary foods become essential.

## Growth while breastfeeding

Healthy breastfed infants typically regain birth weight by about 2 weeks and grow rapidly in the first months. WHO Child Growth Standards, which are based on breastfed infants, are the reference for monitoring growth from birth to 5 years.

## Breastfeeding and maternal medication or illness

Very few maternal conditions or medicines require stopping breastfeeding. A clinician should be consulted about specific medications; in most cases breastfeeding can continue safely.
