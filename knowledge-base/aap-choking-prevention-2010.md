# AAP Policy Statement: Prevention of Choking Among Children (2010)

Applies to: infants and children from birth to about 4 years (0–48 months), the ages at highest risk. Source: American Academy of Pediatrics, Committee on Injury, Violence, and Poison Prevention, *Prevention of Choking Among Children*, Pediatrics, 2010.

## Why young children choke

Children under 4 have small airways, are still learning to chew and swallow, have incomplete molars, explore with their mouths, and are easily distracted while eating. Food is the most common cause of fatal choking in young children, and a food that is round, firm, smooth or sticky and about the width of a child's airway is the most dangerous shape.

## High-risk foods to avoid for children under 4

- **Hot dogs** and sausages (whole or cut into round coins)
- **Whole grapes**, cherries and cherry tomatoes
- **Nuts and seeds** of any kind, including whole peanuts
- **Hard, gooey or sticky candy**, gum, jelly beans, gummy sweets and marshmallows
- **Popcorn**
- **Chunks of meat or cheese**
- **Raw hard vegetables** such as whole raw carrots, celery sticks and raw peas
- **Chunks or spoonfuls of thick peanut butter** or other nut butters
- **Whole raw apple chunks** and other hard fruit pieces
- **Chewing gum**

## Making foods safer

- **Cut round foods lengthwise** into thin strips, then into small pieces: grapes into quarters, hot dogs into thin lengthwise strips (or avoid them).
- **Cook hard vegetables** until soft; grate raw carrot or apple for toddlers.
- **Remove seeds, pits and bones** from fruit, fish and meat.
- **Spread nut butters thinly** on bread or cracker, or thin them into purees; never give a spoonful.
- **Cut food into pieces no larger than about ½ inch (1 cm)** for toddlers; offer soft, mashable pieces to infants learning to self-feed.
- Serve small amounts at a time.

## Safe eating habits

- Children should **always sit upright** to eat — never eat while lying down, walking, running, playing or riding in a car.
- **An adult should watch children while they eat.** Most choking is silent.
- Do not let a child eat while distracted by screens or while laughing or talking with a full mouth.
- Older children should not share snacks with younger siblings unsupervised.
- Do not force a child to eat, and do not rush meals.

## Infant feeding textures

From about 6 months infants can manage smooth purees, then thicker mashed foods with soft lumps, then soft finger foods that dissolve or squash easily between the fingers (well-cooked vegetable pieces, soft ripe fruit, toast strips, soft cheese). Progress textures gradually; a child who gags on lumps is learning, but a child who cannot manage textures expected for age should be assessed.

## Non-food choking hazards

Coins, latex balloons, small balls and marbles, small toy parts, button batteries, magnets and pen caps are the leading non-food items. Balloons are the most common cause of non-food choking death. Keep small objects out of reach and follow toy age labels.

## Be prepared

Parents and caregivers should learn first aid for choking (back blows and chest thrusts for infants under 1 year; abdominal thrusts for children over 1 year) and infant/child CPR. Call emergency services if a child cannot breathe, cough or make sound.
