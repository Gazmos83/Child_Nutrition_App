# AAP Clinical Report: Prevention of Rickets and Vitamin D Deficiency (2008)

Applies to: infants and children from birth through early childhood (0–60 months and beyond). Source: American Academy of Pediatrics, Wagner CL, Greer FR, Section on Breastfeeding and Committee on Nutrition, *Prevention of Rickets and Vitamin D Deficiency in Infants, Children, and Adolescents*, Pediatrics, 2008; intake for children over 1 year updated to match the 2011 Dietary Reference Intakes.

## Why vitamin D matters

Vitamin D is needed to absorb calcium and phosphorus and to build bone. Severe deficiency in infancy causes rickets — soft, deformed bones, delayed growth and, in extreme cases, seizures from low calcium. Vitamin D is made in the skin with sunlight, but infants should be kept out of direct sun, sunscreen blocks synthesis, and skin pigmentation and latitude reduce production, so diet or a supplement must supply it.

## Recommended intake

- **All infants from birth to 12 months: 400 IU (10 micrograms) of vitamin D per day**, beginning in the first few days of life.
- **Children 1 year and older: 600 IU (15 micrograms) per day** (Recommended Dietary Allowance, Institute of Medicine 2011).

## Breastfed infants

Human milk contains very little vitamin D (typically under 25–50 IU per litre), so **breastfed and partially breastfed infants should receive a supplement of 400 IU per day starting in the first days of life**, continued until the infant is weaned to at least 1 litre (about 32 ounces) per day of vitamin D-fortified formula or, after 12 months, whole milk. Liquid infant vitamin D drops are used; parents should follow the dose on the product exactly, because concentrations differ between brands.

## Formula-fed infants

Infant formula in the United States is fortified with about 400 IU of vitamin D per litre. Formula-fed infants who drink **less than 1 litre (32 oz) per day** do not reach 400 IU from formula alone and should also receive a 400 IU supplement until their intake reaches that amount.

## Toddlers and preschoolers

After 12 months, vitamin D comes from fortified cow's milk (about 100 IU per cup/240 ml), fortified soy milk, fortified cereals, egg yolks and fatty fish such as salmon. A child who drinks less than about 4 cups of fortified milk a day, or who does not consume dairy, generally needs a supplement to reach 600 IU. Fortified plant milks other than soy are not nutritionally equivalent to cow's milk for young children and should be discussed with a clinician.

## Who is at extra risk

Exclusively breastfed infants without a supplement, infants with darker skin, infants living at high latitude or in winter, children with fat-malabsorption conditions, and children on certain anticonvulsant medicines are at higher risk of deficiency and may need testing or higher doses under medical supervision.

## Upper limits

Vitamin D is fat-soluble and excess accumulates. Tolerable upper intake levels are 1000 IU/day for 0–6 months, 1500 IU/day for 7–12 months, 2500 IU/day for 1–3 years and 3000 IU/day for 4–8 years. Doses above the standard 400/600 IU should only be given on a clinician's advice.
