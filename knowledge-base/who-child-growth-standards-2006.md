# WHO Child Growth Standards (2006)

Applies to: children from birth to 60 months (5 years). Source: World Health Organization, *WHO Child Growth Standards: length/height-for-age, weight-for-age, weight-for-length, weight-for-height and body mass index-for-age*, 2006.

## What the standards are

The WHO Child Growth Standards describe how children *should* grow when their needs are met — they were built from the WHO Multicentre Growth Reference Study of healthy breastfed children from six countries raised in optimal conditions. They are a prescriptive standard, not a description of average growth in any one population, and they apply to children of all ethnic backgrounds.

The standards cover birth to 5 years and include curves for:

- **Weight-for-age**
- **Length-for-age** (birth to 2 years, measured lying down) and **height-for-age** (2 to 5 years, measured standing)
- **Weight-for-length** and **weight-for-height**
- **Body mass index (BMI)-for-age**
- Head circumference, arm circumference, and skinfolds

Separate curves exist for boys and girls because growth patterns differ by sex.

## Z-scores and percentiles

Each measurement is compared with the standard for the child's exact age (in months and days) and sex and expressed as a **z-score** (standard deviations from the median) or a **percentile**. A z-score of 0 is the median; −2 corresponds to roughly the 2.3rd percentile and +2 to roughly the 97.7th percentile. Because age changes growth expectations so quickly in infancy, age must be recorded precisely — in months, or months and days — rather than in whole years.

## Cut-offs used to classify growth

| Indicator | Z-score below −3 | Z-score below −2 | Z-score above +2 | Z-score above +3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Length/height-for-age | Severely stunted | Stunted | — | — |
| Weight-for-age | Severely underweight | Underweight | — | — |
| Weight-for-length/height | Severely wasted | Wasted | Overweight | Obese |
| BMI-for-age | Severely wasted | Wasted | Overweight | Obese |

A child whose weight-for-age is above +2 may be tall rather than overweight; weight-for-length/height or BMI-for-age should be used to assess overweight.

## Interpreting a single measurement versus a trend

A single measurement places a child on the curve; a *series* of measurements shows the growth trajectory, which is far more informative. Steady growth along a percentile — even a low one — is usually reassuring. Crossing two major percentile lines downward, or a flat weight curve over several months, warrants clinical assessment.

## Breastfed infants and the standards

Because the standards are based on breastfed infants, the growth of healthy breastfed babies tracks the curves well. Breastfed infants tend to gain weight more slowly after 3–4 months than formula-fed infants; on older references this looked like faltering, but on the WHO standards it is normal.

## Body mass index in early childhood

BMI is weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. In children BMI must always be interpreted against the age- and sex-specific curve, never against adult cut-offs. BMI-for-age is available from birth on the WHO standards; many national programmes begin using BMI-for-age routinely at 2 years, when standing height is measured.

## How the standards are used

Health workers plot each child's measurements at every visit to detect undernutrition (stunting, wasting, underweight) and overweight early. Growth monitoring is one of the core reasons for regular well-child visits in the first five years.
