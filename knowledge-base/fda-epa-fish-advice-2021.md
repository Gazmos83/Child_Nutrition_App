# FDA/EPA Advice About Eating Fish for Young Children (2021)

Applies to: children from about 6 months (as a complementary food) through 60 months, with the serving-size advice beginning at age 2. Source: U.S. Food and Drug Administration and U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, *Advice About Eating Fish: For Those Who Might Become or Are Pregnant or Breastfeeding and Children Ages 1–11 Years*, revised October 2021.

## Why fish is recommended for children

Fish and shellfish supply high-quality protein, iron, zinc, iodine, vitamin B12, vitamin D and the omega-3 fatty acids DHA and EPA, which are important for brain and eye development. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans encourage seafood as a complementary food from about 6 months and 2 servings a week from age 2. The advice below balances those benefits against exposure to mercury, which is higher in some species.

## Serving sizes by age

A serving for a child is smaller than for an adult and grows with age:

- **Age 1–3 years:** about **1 ounce (28 g)** per serving
- **Age 4–7 years:** about **2 ounces (57 g)** per serving
- (Age 8–10: 3 ounces; 11 and older: 4 ounces)

For infants 6–12 months, fish is introduced as a complementary food — flaked, boneless, well cooked — in amounts appropriate to the infant's meal size; it is also one of the allergenic foods to introduce early rather than delay.

## How often

Children should eat fish **1 to 2 times per week**, choosing from the "Best Choices" list, with serving sizes as above.

## Best Choices (eat 2 servings a week)

Lower-mercury fish and shellfish suitable for young children include: **salmon, cod, haddock, pollock, tilapia, catfish, flounder and sole, trout (freshwater), whitefish, sardines, anchovies, herring, canned light tuna (skipjack), shrimp, crab, scallops, clams, oysters (cooked), Atlantic mackerel, and Pacific chub mackerel**.

## Good Choices (limit to 1 serving a week)

Bluefish, grouper, halibut, mahi mahi, monkfish, snapper, Spanish mackerel, striped bass, **albacore/white tuna (canned, fresh or frozen)**, yellowfin tuna, tilefish (Atlantic Ocean), Chilean sea bass, rockfish, sablefish, carp, buffalofish, sheepshead, weakfish, white croaker.

## Choices to avoid (highest mercury)

**King mackerel, marlin, orange roughy, shark, swordfish, tilefish (Gulf of Mexico), and bigeye tuna.** These should not be served to young children.

## Preparation and safety

- Serve fish **fully cooked** to an internal temperature of 145°F (63°C); flesh should be opaque and flake easily. **Raw or undercooked fish and shellfish, including sushi, ceviche and smoked seafood that is not cooked, are not safe for young children.**
- **Remove all bones** and check carefully; flake fish finely for infants and toddlers.
- Fish sticks and breaded products count, but check sodium and choose those made from whole fillets.
- Canned fish with bones (sardines, salmon) has soft, edible bones that add calcium — mash well.

## Fish caught by family or friends

Check local advisories for lakes and rivers. If there is no advisory, limit locally caught fish to one serving a week and do not serve other fish that week.

## Allergy note

Fish and shellfish are among the common food allergens. Introduce them like other allergenic foods — one at a time, early in the day, watching for reactions — and seek advice from a clinician if a reaction occurs.
