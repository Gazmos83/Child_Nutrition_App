# Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020–2025 — Infants and Toddlers (Birth to 24 Months)

Applies to: infants and toddlers from birth to 24 months. Source: U.S. Department of Agriculture and U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, *Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2020–2025*, Chapter 2: Infants and Toddlers, December 2020. This is the first edition of the Guidelines to include recommendations for children under 2.

## Feeding in the first 6 months

- Feed infants **human milk exclusively for about the first 6 months**. Continue to feed human milk through at least the first year, and longer if desired.
- If human milk is unavailable, feed **iron-fortified infant formula** during the first year.
- Give infants **a vitamin D supplement (400 IU/day) beginning soon after birth** if fed human milk (or if formula intake is under about 32 oz/day).

## Introducing complementary foods

- Introduce **nutrient-dense complementary foods at about 6 months**. Do not introduce before 4 months. Signs of developmental readiness accompany age.
- **Introduce potentially allergenic foods along with other complementary foods.** Introducing peanut-containing foods in the first year reduces the risk of peanut allergy; there is no evidence that delaying egg, dairy, wheat, soy, fish, shellfish or tree nuts prevents allergy.
- Encourage a **variety of foods from all food groups**: vegetables, fruits, grains (whole grains where possible), protein foods (meat, poultry, seafood, eggs, beans, peas, lentils, nuts and seeds in safe forms) and dairy (yoghurt, cheese; fortified soy alternatives).
- Emphasise foods rich in **iron and zinc** — meats, seafood, iron-fortified cereals, beans — particularly for infants fed human milk, whose iron stores decline after about 6 months.
- Include **seafood** as a complementary food, choosing lower-mercury options (see FDA/EPA advice); it supplies omega-3 fatty acids important for brain development.

## Added sugars, sodium and drinks

- **Avoid foods and beverages with added sugars entirely for children younger than 2 years.** The Guidelines' nutrient needs for this age leave no room for added sugars.
- **Limit foods higher in sodium** — many processed foods and snacks marketed for toddlers are high in sodium; the Chronic Disease Risk Reduction intake for 1–3 years is 1,200 mg/day.
- Infants under 12 months should drink **human milk or infant formula**; small amounts of plain water may be offered from about 6 months. **Avoid juice, sugar-sweetened beverages, and cow's milk as a drink before 12 months.**
- From 12 months, **plain whole milk** and plain water are the everyday drinks.

## The 12–23 month dietary pattern

Toddlers 12–23 months who are no longer fed human milk or formula need **roughly 700 to 1,000 calories per day**, depending on size and activity. A healthy pattern in this range includes, per day, about:

- **⅔ to 1 cup vegetables**, varied across the week (dark green, red and orange, beans/peas/lentils, starchy, other)
- **½ to 1 cup fruits**
- **1¾ to 3 ounce-equivalents of grains**, at least half whole grains
- **1⅔ to 2 cups dairy** (whole milk, yoghurt, cheese; fortified soy)
- **2 ounce-equivalents of protein foods**, including seafood 2–3 ounces per week
- **9 to 13 grams of oils**

Toddlers still receiving human milk need proportionally less from foods. Amounts are targets over the day, not per meal, and appetite varies widely from day to day.

## Nutrients of concern

- **Iron and zinc** for infants around 6–12 months, especially those fed human milk.
- **Vitamin D** across the whole age range.
- **Potassium, fibre and choline** are often low in the diets of toddlers 12–23 months; vegetables, fruits, beans and eggs help.
- **Vitamin B12** for infants of mothers following vegan diets.

## Responsive feeding and routine

Establish predictable meal and snack times, let the toddler decide how much to eat, avoid pressure and food rewards, and offer new foods repeatedly. Eating together and modelling varied food choices shape preferences that persist into later childhood.
