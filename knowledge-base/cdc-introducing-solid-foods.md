# CDC: When, What, and How to Introduce Solid Foods

Applies to: infants and toddlers from about 4 to 24 months. Source: Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, *Infant and Toddler Nutrition — Foods and Drinks: When, What, and How to Introduce Solid Foods*, 2024; consistent with the Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020–2025 and American Academy of Pediatrics guidance.

## When to start

Most babies are ready for foods other than breast milk or infant formula at **about 6 months of age**. Starting before 4 months is not recommended. Signs of readiness — which should be present together with the age — include:

- Sits up alone or with support and holds the head steady
- Opens the mouth or leans forward when food is offered
- Swallows food rather than pushing it back out with the tongue
- Brings objects to the mouth and tries to grasp small items

Wanting to nurse or bottle-feed more often, or waking at night, on its own is not a sign that a baby needs solids.

## What to offer first

There is no required order. Good first foods are **iron- and zinc-rich**: iron-fortified infant cereal (oat, barley, multigrain — not rice cereal exclusively, to limit arsenic exposure), pureed meat, poultry, fish, beans and lentils. Also offer pureed or mashed vegetables and fruits, and full-fat plain yoghurt. Introduce **one new single-ingredient food at a time, waiting a few days** between new foods to spot any reaction.

**Potentially allergenic foods** — peanut (as thinned smooth peanut butter), egg (cooked), cow's milk products (yoghurt, cheese), soy, wheat, fish, shellfish, tree nuts (ground or as butter), sesame — should be introduced along with other complementary foods from about 6 months; there is no benefit to delaying them. Infants with severe eczema or an existing food allergy should have a clinician's guidance first.

## Textures and self-feeding

- **About 6 months:** smooth purees and very soft mashed foods, thick enough to stay on a spoon.
- **7–8 months:** thicker mashed foods with soft lumps; start soft finger foods the baby can pick up and that squash easily (well-cooked vegetable pieces, ripe banana, avocado, soft cheese, toast strips).
- **9–12 months:** finely chopped soft foods, small pieces of soft cooked meat, family foods mashed or cut small; the baby increasingly self-feeds with fingers and begins using a spoon and open cup.
- **12–24 months:** family foods cut into pieces no bigger than about ½ inch, with continued attention to choking hazards.

## How much and how often

Start with 1–2 tablespoons once or twice a day and follow the baby's cues, increasing to 2–3 meals a day by 8–9 months and 3 meals plus 1–2 snacks by 12 months. Breast milk or formula remains the main source of nutrition through the first year; solids complement it. Let the baby decide how much to eat — never force-feed or insist on finishing.

## Drinks

From about 6 months, small sips of **plain water** — about 4–8 ounces (½–1 cup) a day — can be offered in an open cup with meals. **No juice before 12 months, no cow's milk as a drink before 12 months**, no sugary or flavoured drinks.

## What not to give before 12 months

- **Honey** (infant botulism risk)
- **Cow's milk as a drink** or unfortified plant milks (low iron; wait until 12 months)
- **Unpasteurised milk, juice or cheese**; raw or undercooked eggs, meat, fish, sprouts
- **Foods with added sugar** and **high-sodium foods** (processed meats, salty snacks)
- **Choking hazards**: whole grapes, whole nuts, popcorn, hot dogs, hard candy, raw hard vegetables, large chunks of any food
- **Fish high in mercury** (king mackerel, shark, swordfish, tilefish, bigeye tuna, marlin, orange roughy)

## Feeding responsively

Sit facing the baby, respond to hunger and fullness cues, allow mess and exploration, and offer new foods repeatedly — it can take 8–10 or more exposures before a baby accepts a new taste. A refusal today is not a permanent dislike.
